Your LLM is a Drunken Horse

Tremendous power. Zero direction. One glass of scotch too many. Give it the reins and it'll trot straight into a hallucination.

I've been working with LLMs long enough to develop a very precise mental model for what they are. Not a calculator. Not a search engine. Not a sentient oracle. A drunken horse.

Hear me out. It's not an insult. The horse is magnificent — trained on the entire library of human language, mathematics, code, and poetry. It has raw, breathtaking capability. But it also had one too many pegs of single malt. And right now it's standing in your data pipeline, looking very confident, facing entirely the wrong direction.

"The power was never the problem. The direction always was."

The Stable Before the Storm

Think about what a horse actually is before the scotch enters the picture. Thousands of hours of training. Muscle memory. Pattern recognition. The ability to carry enormous weight at speed, navigate terrain, respond to pressure. LLMs are trained on tokens — billions of them — and they build a similar kind of compressed, probabilistic muscle memory for language and logic.

The training is real. The capability is real. GPT-4, Claude, Gemini — these are not toys. They can debug production code, synthesize research papers, draft legal clauses, and explain Kubernetes networking to a junior engineer at 3 AM. The horse is strong.

Then someone left the scotch cabinet open.

The scotch — statistically speaking — is the nature of next-token prediction. The model doesn't have goals. It doesn't have memory of the task. It doesn't have a map. It has extraordinary fluency and no compass. Every token is a bet on what comes next, taken in context of what just came before. High-temperature sampling is literally turning up the scotch dial.

Giving It the Lagam (Reins)

A word on language before we go further. Reins in English is just the physical strap — a tool, neutral, mechanical. Lagam (लगाम) in Hindi carries the act itself: disciplined control over something powerful. Bina lagam ka ghoda — a horse without lagam — is already an idiom for unchecked force. So when we say lagam (reins), we mean not just the strap but the intention of control. Free reins = no lagam = chaos. Lagam applied = reins with purpose = a thoroughbred that wins races. This is the whole game of prompt engineering, retrieval augmentation, fine-tuning, and agent design. Every technique we've built in the last three years is a version of the same thing: putting a harness on a very powerful animal that has questionable judgment.

The sober engineer's job is not to sober the horse up. You can't. The stochastic nature is baked in. Your job is to design a track it cannot escape from.

🎯 The LAGAM Framework — Engineering Control Over LLMs
L
Lock the output format. JSON schemas, XML tags, typed responses. Don't ask the horse to decide how to present the answer. It will add fourteen sentences of context nobody asked for.
A
Anchor to ground truth. RAG isn't magic — it's giving the horse a map it cannot ignore. Retrieval grounds generation. Without it, memory is imagination.
G
Gate every step. Agentic systems are multi-hop drunk-horse parades. Each hop needs a gate — a validator, a guardrail, a confidence check. One hallucination compounds into seven.
A
Audit the reasoning, not just the output. Ask the model to show its work via Chain-of-Thought. A wrong answer with visible reasoning is debuggable. A confident wrong answer is a production incident.
M
Minimise the blast radius. Single-responsibility prompts. One task per call. The more instructions on the horse's back, the more likely it throws them all off and trots away cheerfully.

The Prompt Is the Bridle

Here's what most people get wrong: they write prompts like they're filing a support ticket. Vague, hopeful, underspecified. And then they're surprised when the horse doesn't know which barn to go to.

❌ No lagam — open field, scotch in hand
"Analyze my sales data and give me insights."
✅ Lagam applied — track, direction, fence posts
"You are a data analyst. The user will provide a CSV of monthly sales data.
Your task: identify the top 3 revenue trends from the past 6 months.
Format: JSON array with keys: trend_name, evidence, recommendation.
Do NOT include projections. Do NOT reference data outside the provided CSV.
If data is insufficient, return: {"error": "insufficient_data", "reason": <string>}"

The second prompt isn't longer because the engineer was more verbose. It's longer because the engineer built a fence. Role, task, scope, format, constraint, and a graceful failure mode. The horse has nowhere to go but forward.

temp=1.5 — Full drunken horse 🐴Poetry? Chaos? Genius?

For production data systems, keep the horse at two pegs maximum. For creative work, let it have the whole bottle and then edit sober. The mistake is using high temperature for tasks that require precision and low temperature for tasks that require creativity. Know your task first.

When You Drop the Lagam (Reins)

Here's the uncomfortable truth that nobody wants to say at the enterprise AI conference: most LLM failures in production are human failures of specification. The horse didn't break free. We dropped the lagam. We handed it a map of a different city and were surprised when it got lost.

Now here's an interesting observation — and this is where the drunken horse metaphor gets precise. After a few pegs of scotch, the horse becomes a bak bak machine — a chatterbox. It talks. Fluently. Endlessly. Eloquently. And this is actually its natural strength: language is what the LLM was trained on. Ask it to summarise, explain, translate, rephrase, draft, narrate — and the scotch barely shows. The horse is in its element, verbal and warm. RAG tasks are relatively easy to rein in because the horse is already a language animal — you give it a document, it talks about the document. The lagam is light.

But ask it to solve a structural problem — write a complex SQL query with multi-level CTEs, handle a self-join across three tables, apply window functions with the right PARTITION BY logic — and watch what happens. The horse hallucinates column names that don't exist, invents table aliases, produces a query that looks syntactically perfect and is logically wrong in a way that only fails at runtime on edge cases. The bak bak continues, but now it's confident nonsense. This is where the lagam matters most. Prompt guardrails, schema injection, output validators, test-case gates — the full harness, not just a loose strap.

Agentic AI — where the LLM makes decisions, calls tools, loops on its own output — is a drunken horse with the stable door open. It can do extraordinary things. I've built 40-plus test cases on multi-agent systems, and I will tell you with complete sincerity: the moments of brilliance are real, and they require infrastructure-grade controls around them to not become moments of elegant, confident catastrophe.

"Give it free reins (no lagam) and you're gone for toss. Give it the lagam (reins, with intention) and it can do wonders."

The most valuable skill in applied AI right now is not knowing which model to use. It's knowing how to write a harness. System prompts, schema contracts, retrieval architecture, guardrail layers, fallback handlers. The boring stuff. The infrastructure of control.

The horse is ready. The scotch was always going to be there. The question is whether you're a rider or a spectator.

🥃 "The best prompt engineers are not writers. They're stable managers."
